01

STIX Luxury

● Live

An online storefront for a senator-wear tailoring house in Douala. Customers browse the collection in English or French, upload a full-body photo and see themselves actually wearing an outfit before they commit, then place the order straight into the shop's WhatsApp with every detail filled in — items, colours, sizes, total.

The hard part: AI image generation costs money per use, so an open try-on button is an open wallet. I built a signed-cookie quota system that gives each customer three previews a month and caps the whole shop daily, enforced on the server where it cannot be edited by the visitor. Failed generations refund the customer's attempt automatically.

02

FaceSort

Computer vision

Drop a thousand event photos in. FaceSort finds every face, works out which ones are the same person without being given a single reference photo, and hands each guest a folder containing only the pictures they appear in. Guests open one link and a passcode — or just take a selfie and let it find them. Albums delete themselves after a set number of days.

The hard part: grouping faces with no labels to learn from, then letting a human correct it without retraining anything — not this person, it's someone else, merge these two. Plus running it two ways: a double-click on a laptop for an event tonight, or Docker with automatic HTTPS for a permanent server.

03

Saint Mary Campus Services

● In operation

Not a demo — a real service business on a university campus, run entirely from one website I built. Three services share it: delivery to hostels and staff quarters with booked day and hour slots, a laundry service with prepaid plans that count themselves down and live progress a student can watch move from collected to washing to ready, and room cleaning where each time slot can be taken by exactly one room.

The hard part: the users are students and the operators are students, so nothing could require explanation. Mobile Money payment with a downloadable receipt, an admin panel someone taps between classes, and database rules strict enough that a curious student cannot read another person's laundry account.

04

Douala AI

Language preservation

A chatbot that holds a conversation in English, French and Duala — the language of the Douala people. Most AI models have effectively never seen Duala, because almost none of it exists in digital form. So I made some: I ran OCR across a scanned 1972 Helmlinger Duala–French dictionary and turned a paper book into something a model could actually use.

The hard part: a language with no training data is not a prompting problem, it is a sourcing problem. Getting clean text out of a fifty-year-old scanned dictionary, then grounding the model in it so it answers in Duala rather than inventing words that sound like Duala.

PythonFastAPIClaude APIOCRWeb search
05

Motion Studio AI

Creative tooling

A motion-graphics studio that runs inside a browser tab with nothing installed and no internet needed. Describe the animation, adjust it on a timeline, then export it with genuine transparency — ProRes 4444 or a PNG sequence — so it drops into DaVinci Resolve and sits over whatever footage is underneath.

The hard part: transparency. Normal browser video export flattens the alpha channel and editors show a black box instead of a clean edge. Getting a true alpha channel out of a web page, in a format a professional editor accepts, is most of the work in this project.

JavaScriptthree.jsWebCodecsProRes 4444
06

Base Global Logistics — Company Portfolio

● In use

During my first-year industrial attachment at Base Global Logistics Ltd, a freight-forwarding and supply-chain company in Douala, I was given the company portfolio to build: the piece the business uses to present its air and sea cargo, customs clearance, warehousing and distribution services to clients. I built it independently, took it through rounds of review with the Head of Sales and Customer Service, and it was approved and adopted by the company.

The hard part: it was not my business and not my voice. I had to learn how a logistics company actually operates from the field first, then keep revising until the people who sell those services every day said it represented them correctly.

About

Who is building this

I'm Ty Asher Ringnyu Ginti Njo, a developer in Douala, Cameroon, studying for a Bachelor of Engineering in Data Science and AI Engineering at Saint Mary’s University Institute. I build software the way a tailor works: the thing has to fit the person who ordered it, and it has to survive being worn.

Most of what I make starts as somebody's actual complaint. A tailor losing sales because customers cannot picture themselves in the outfit. A photographer spending a weekend sorting event photos by hand. Students with nowhere to send their laundry. I would rather ship something ordinary that someone uses every week than something clever that sits in a folder.

The AI work interests me most where it meets things that are local and overlooked — a language with no training data, a shop with no online presence, a payment method the big platforms ignore. That ground is wide open here, and almost nobody is building on it.

I'm open to freelance and contract work: web applications, AI integrations, e-commerce, and automation for businesses that need the practical version rather than the expensive one.
